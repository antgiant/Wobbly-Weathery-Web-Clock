## 1. Weather (asked 2026-11-07; the foundation comes first)

All of these are part of the "weather" rung of the capability ladder: they must switch off on weak hardware like rain does (the Zero drops weather first). Tornado graphics are **not** wanted (removed on request); tornado *notices* are (item 2).

1. **Foundation: extend the weather model** (`js/sky/weather.js` PRESETS and `mapOpenMeteo`): sleet, freezing rain/drizzle, hail, lightning, dust, rime, gusts; live mapping from Open-Meteo (WMO codes 48, 56, 57, 66, 67, 96, 99; wind gusts; air-quality `dust`; check whether CAPE or lightning potential is offered); manual `weatherMode` choices (`schema/settings.js`, the frozen share-link table in `js/share.js` gets a new version, `schema/settings.json` and the man page are regenerated); the rain alert text (`js/sky/rain.js`); unit tests.
2. **Wind tilts the rain and blows the snow** (scene and the glass-digit drops in `js/sky/drops.js`; blowing snow near the ground in strong wind).
3. **Freezing rain and freezing drizzle** (and sleet, ice pellets): drops freeze on the glass digits into beads and sheets, glaze on the ground and plants (shine, bend, icicles); also the **sleet** item (rain and snow mixed, today the larger of the two wins).
4. **Hail**: stones that bounce off the glass digits (drops physics) and the ground, litter on the ground.
5. **Lightning**: random strikes (ideally rate from reality: thunderstorm codes 95 to 99, plus lightning data if the forecast service has it), flash of sky and cloud, also on the digits.
6. **Freezing fog and rime** (code 48 and fog below freezing): rime on plants, ground and the digits' edges.
7. **Dust and sand storms**: orange-brown haze, low visibility, drifting sand; live from air-quality dust and wind; landscape-aware (desert).
8. **Rainbows, based on reality**: sun up to about 42 degrees, rain falling or just ending on the other side of the sky, sun not hidden; a 42 degree ring round the antisolar point, faint outer bow at 51 degrees.
9. **Aurora, based on reality**: at night, clear enough sky, the place's geomagnetic latitude against the current Kp index (NOAA SWPC planetary K; check it can be fetched from a web page and from the Pi); curtains toward the pole; manual choice too.

What the clock draws today and what it cannot (checked 2026-11-07, `js/sky/weather.js`): cloud cover in three layers, rain, snow (and snow on the ground), fog, smoky haze, thunderstorm as a dark sky with heavy rain, wind (clouds and plants only).

## 3. Look and landscape (asked 2026-11-07)

- **A better city skyline** (`js/sky/landscape.js`, the terrain shader in `js/sky/glsl-scene.js`).
- **Plant life by location**: the existing trees suit some places and look wrong in others; choose species and palette from the place (latitude, climate, coast, desert...) in `js/sky/biome.js` and the plants shaders; the user's own choice still wins.
- **Seasonal trees and foliage (asked 2026-11-07)**: tree colours and foliage should be right for the time of year, ideally matching real life at the clock's location: leaves greening in spring, full in summer, turning (yellow, orange, red) and falling in autumn, bare in winter for deciduous trees; evergreens, palms and desert plants stay as they are; the southern hemisphere is flipped; the tropics and dry seasons follow rainfall, not the calendar. Start from what exists (`seasonOf` and `seasonFromClimate` in `js/sky/biome.js`, which already use the date, hemisphere and the last month's temperature, and `species(b, season)` in `js/sky/plants.js`, which today only uses season for flowers). Make it a gradual change through the season (a leaf-colour fraction from the date, latitude and recent temperature) rather than four jumps, check it with the simulated time (`js/simtime.js`) at several places and dates, and add a test.
- **Big sun and moon near the horizon (asked 2026-11-07)**: replicate the "moon illusion" look, where the sun and moon seem extra large when low in the sky. Two separate things, do them in this order: (1) the real, physical part: refraction squashes the disc vertically near the horizon (the lower limb is lifted about 0.1 degree more than the upper, so the disc is about 0.15 degree flatter at 0 degrees altitude; use the same Bennett formula the stars use in `js/sky/glsl-scene.js`, applied to the disc's top and bottom edge, in the sun and moon passes) and the reddening and dimming already come from the atmosphere model; (2) the perceptual part: a real horizon sun is the same size as a high one, it only looks bigger, so to copy that look add a size boost that grows as altitude falls (for example `bodySize * (1 + k * smoothstep(30, 0, alt))`, k about 0.3 to 0.6, tunable) applied where `uSunAngR` and `uMoonAngR` are set (`js/sky/engine.js` about line 819), gated by a new setting (the "Realistic" size must switch it off) in `schema/settings.js`, and then regenerate `schema/settings.json` and the man page, and add the share-link table entry. Check: sun and moon at altitudes 0, 5, 15, 30, 60 on the simulated time (`js/simtime.js`); the boost must be smooth, never jump as the body crosses the horizon, and cost nothing on the Pi's low rungs.
- **Verify the positions of stars, moon and planets (asked 2026-11-07)**: read from the code, they should be right (see below), but there is no test that checks them against an independent source. Add a unit test (`tests/unit`) of `computeSky` (`js/sky/astro.js`) against published almanac values (for example the US Naval Observatory or JPL Horizons altitude and azimuth of the sun, moon, Venus, Jupiter and a few bright stars such as Sirius, Vega and Polaris, at two places and three dates, tolerance about 0.1 degree), plus a pixel check that the star shader's refracted star and the refracted sun agree at the horizon. What the code does today: sun, moon and planets come from astronomy-engine as topocentric apparent positions with refraction (`bodyHorizon`: `Equator(..., ofdate, aberration)` then `Horizon(..., 'normal')`); stars use `Rotation_EQJ_HOR` (J2000 to the horizon frame for the date and place) and the shader adds Bennett refraction itself, so stars and the sun are shifted the same way. Things to check: the star catalogue's epoch and whether proper motion is applied (small, under 0.05 degree for bright stars over decades); the moon's libration and pole orientation; that the camera's azimuth convention (north towards east) is the same in the sky, the labels and the landscape (the sun must set behind the hill that is really to the west).
